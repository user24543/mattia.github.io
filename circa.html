( 01 · how the clock works )

01 / 04

light sets a master clock in your brain, and it keeps the rest of you in time.

chronobiology

the study of timing in living things: daily rhythms, tides, seasons and the yearly cycles of plants and animals. the circadian rhythm, from the latin circa diem, "about a day", is its best-known subject.

1 · light

the eye

a small set of cells in the retina contain melanopsin, a pigment most sensitive to blue light. they don't help you see; they measure how bright the day is.

2 · the master clock

the scn

the suprachiasmatic nucleus, about 20,000 neurons above the crossing of the optic nerves. on its own it ticks with a period a little over 24 hours; the light signal nudges it into step with the sun.

3 · the signal

melatonin

the scn tells the pineal gland when to release melatonin. it rises about two hours before your usual bedtime and is shut off by bright light.

4 · the body

everything else

body temperature, hormones, digestion and alertness follow along. your temperature bottoms out about two hours before you usually wake.

an actogram, like the one in the panel, is how chronobiologists draw this. each row is one day, plotted twice side by side so a night that crosses midnight stays in one piece. when the clock is in step, sleep stacks into a straight column. when it drifts, the column leans.

the model behind it is deliberately small: one clock with its own period, and a rule for how light moves it. light in the evening and early night pushes the clock later; light in the early morning pulls it earlier. that rule, the phase response curve, is the core idea of how light sets the clock.

( 02 · the loop inside each cell )

the clock is a set of genes that switch themselves off.

per · cry rnaprotein in the cellprotein back in the nucleus

two proteins, clock and bmal1, pair up and switch on the period and cryptochrome genes. their messages leave the nucleus and are made into per and cry proteins. those build up for hours, pair up, move back into the nucleus and block clock and bmal1, switching off their own genes. once they break down, the genes switch on again. one turn takes about a day.

the delays in each step are what make it slow. jeffrey hall, michael rosbash and michael young worked out this loop in fruit flies and shared the 2017 nobel prize for it.

( 03 · without the sun )

deep underground, with no daylight, the human day stretches past 24 hours.

~48 hhow long some of his "days" lasted during six months in a texas cave in 1972
40 daysthe deep time experiment in a french cave in 2021; the team guessed about 30

in 1962 the french speleologist michel siffre lived alone in a cave in the alps for about two months, with no clock and no daylight, phoning the surface each time he woke, ate and slept. when he was brought out he was sure far less time had passed than really had. his days had quietly grown longer than 24 hours.

in 1972 he spent about six months in midnight cave in texas. his sleep and waking drifted later every day, and at times a single cycle stretched to around 48 hours.

in bunkers in germany in the 1960s, jürgen aschoff and rütger wever found the same thing: without time cues, people free-run on days of roughly 25 hours, and the sleep cycle can even split away from the body-temperature cycle. later, more careful lab work put the clock itself at about 24.2 hours on average; the lamp people switch on while awake pushes it later still.

( 04 · living against the clock )

04 / 04
i.

east is harder

flying east means your clock has to move earlier. most human clocks run a little long, so they find delaying easier than advancing. try both: "fly 8 hours east" above takes several days to settle.

ii.

owls and larks

people differ in when their clock runs, called their chronotype. teenagers drift later through puberty and swing back earlier in their twenties, which is part of why early school starts are hard on them.

iii.

a clock in every cell

the liver, heart, lungs and fat all carry their own copies of the loop. the scn keeps them in step, but meal times can pull the liver's clock on its own.

iv.

light at night

the cells that set the clock respond most to blue light. bright screens and lamps late in the evening suppress melatonin and push the clock later, the same way a lamp does in the cave.
