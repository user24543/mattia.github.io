( 01 · the rule )

01 / 04

on islands, small animals tend to get bigger and big ones smaller.

in 1964 the canadian biologist j. bristol foster compared mammals living on the islands off british columbia with their relatives on the mainland. rodents on the islands were often larger; deer, carnivores and other large mammals were often smaller. leigh van valen later named the pattern the island rule, and it is often called foster's rule.

mark lomolino showed in 1985 that it is a gradient, not two separate tricks. the smallest species grow the most, the largest shrink the most, and somewhere in the middle there is a size that barely changes at all.

the usual explanation has two halves. small animals on islands escape the predators and competitors that kept them small, so being big starts to pay. large animals run into a hard ceiling of food and space, so being small starts to pay.

( 04 · where the rule breaks )

04 / 04
i.

a tendency, not a law

plenty of island species ignore it. in 2008 shai meiri and colleagues found the pattern varies a lot between groups, and argued it is better seen as a set of separate stories than one rule.

ii.

but it does show up

a 2021 analysis of thousands of island and mainland populations found the pattern across mammals, birds and reptiles, strongest on small, remote islands, the same knobs this page lets you turn.

iii.

the dragon that wasn't

the komodo dragon looks like an island giant, but fossils show its relatives were already that big in australia. it may simply be the last of a large line, not something the island made.

iv.

first to go

island giants and dwarfs evolved without people, and many disappeared soon after people arrived: the dodo, the moa, the dwarf hippos. their strangeness was a product of isolation, and isolation is what ended.
