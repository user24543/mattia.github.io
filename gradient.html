( 01 · learning, plainly )

01 / 04

a model is a function with millions of dials. training is the slow work of turning every dial slightly downhill.

a machine learning model takes an input, such as a photo, a sentence or a pair of coordinates, and produces an output. what it produces depends on a very large set of numbers called parameters. at the start they are random, so the answers are nonsense.

training fixes that by repetition. the model sees examples whose answers are already known, a loss function scores how wrong it was, and an algorithm nudges every parameter in the direction that makes that score smaller. after enough rounds the function captures the pattern behind the examples, and it can answer for inputs it has never seen.

the landscape at the top of this page is that idea drawn literally: height is loss, position is the value of two parameters, and the beads are optimisers feeling for the slope.

( 04 · four ideas worth keeping )

04 / 04
i.

overfitting

a model with enough parameters can memorise its training examples instead of learning the rule behind them. it scores well on data it has seen and badly on data it hasn't. that is why models are always judged on a held-out test set.

ii.

learning rate

the size of each step downhill. too small and training crawls; too large and the optimiser overshoots the valley and bounces out of it. most training runs change it over time, starting larger and ending smaller.

iii.

embeddings

models turn words, images and sounds into long lists of numbers, called vectors, arranged so that similar things land close together. "cat" ends up nearer to "kitten" than to "carburettor", and the model can reason with those distances.

iv.

attention

the core of the transformer, the architecture behind most large language models. for every token in a sequence, attention scores how relevant every other token is, then updates that token using a weighted blend of the others.
