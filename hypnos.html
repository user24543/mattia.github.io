    ( 01 · five kinds of wave )

    01 / 05

    a brainwave is millions of neurons pulsing together.

    an electrode on the scalp can't hear single neurons. what it picks up is the summed electrical current of millions of pyramidal cells in the cortex, all lined up the same way. when they work independently, their currents mostly cancel and the signal is small and fast. when they fall into step, they add up into big, slow waves.

    much of the timing comes from loops between the thalamus and the cortex. the thalamus acts as a pacemaker: its neurons can fire in rhythmic bursts that drive spindles and slow waves in sleep, and alpha when you rest with your eyes closed. faster rhythms like gamma come from local circuits of fast inhibitory neurons inside the cortex.

    hans berger recorded the first human eeg in 1924 and published it in 1929. the first rhythm he named was alpha, the one that appears when you close your eyes.

    raise the coupling and the neurons pull each other into step: the same cells, the same activity, but a much bigger wave. this is why the slow waves of deep sleep are the largest signals the brain makes.

    ( 02 · the stages of a night )

    02 / 05

    every night you go down and back up about five times.

    non-rem~5%

    n1

    drifting off, theta

    alpha fades into slower theta, the eyes roll slowly, and muscles relax, sometimes with a sudden hypnic jerk. it lasts minutes and is easy to wake from; people woken here often say they weren't asleep.

    non-rem~45–55%

    n2

    light sleep, spindles

    the signature marks are sleep spindles, half-second bursts at 11–16 hz from the thalamus, and k-complexes, single large waves often set off by a sound. spindles are linked to storing memories and to keeping noise from waking you.

    non-rem~15–25%

    n3

    deep, slow-wave sleep

    big delta waves under 2 hz. the hardest stage to wake from, when growth hormone is released and sleepwalking and night terrors begin. it is concentrated in the first half of the night and shrinks with age.

    rem~20–25%

    rem

    dreaming, theta and sawtooth

    the eeg looks almost awake: low, fast and mixed, with theta and sawtooth waves. the eyes dart under the lids, the body is paralysed apart from the eyes and breathing, and dreams are most vivid. rem periods get longer toward morning.

    a cycle runs from n1 down to n3 and back up into rem, and takes around 90 minutes. early cycles are heavy on deep n3; later ones are mostly n2 and long rem. that is why cutting a night short costs you dreams more than deep sleep.

    the stage names come from the american academy of sleep medicine, which in 2007 merged the old stages 3 and 4 into one n3. sleep labs score a recording in 30-second pages, each given one stage, which is how a hypnogram like the one in the hero is drawn.

    the three lines in the recording above are what a sleep lab actually measures. eeg for the brain, eog for the eyes, and emg for muscle tone at the chin. you need all three to spot rem: the brain looks awake and the eyes are busy, but the chin goes almost silent because the body is switched off.

    in deep sleep, in mice, the space between brain cells widens and waste is washed out faster, one of several reasons slow-wave sleep seems to matter for the brain's upkeep.

    ( 03 · when the stages misbehave )

    03 / 05

    sometimes rem arrives too early, or deep sleep never fully lets go of waking.

    soremp

    sleep-onset rem

    normally the first rem period comes about 90 minutes after you fall asleep. a sleep-onset rem period, or soremp, is rem within 15 minutes of falling asleep. one now and then can follow sleep deprivation; repeated ones are a key sign of narcolepsy.

    narcolepsy

    rem leaking into the day

    affecting about 1 in 2,000 people, narcolepsy brings overwhelming daytime sleepiness. in type 1, most of the few tens of thousands of orexin neurons in the hypothalamus are lost, and pieces of rem break into waking: cataplexy (sudden muscle weakness set off by laughing or surprise), sleep paralysis and vivid hallucinations at the edges of sleep. it is diagnosed with a nap test: falling asleep in 8 minutes or less on average, with at least two soremps.

    alpha-delta sleep

    awake inside deep sleep

    first described in 1973: waking alpha rhythm riding on top of the slow delta waves of deep sleep. people with it often wake unrefreshed, and it has been linked with chronic pain conditions such as fibromyalgia, though it also turns up in healthy sleepers.

    other

    stages that mix

    sleepwalking and night terrors come out of partial waking from n3. in rem sleep behaviour disorder, the paralysis of rem fails and people act out their dreams. sleep paralysis is the opposite: waking up while rem's paralysis is still on.

    ( 04 · binaural beats )

    04 / 05

    two tones, one in each ear, and a third beat that exists only in your head.

    200 hz
    10 hz
    35%

    play 200 hz into the left ear and 210 hz into the right. neither ear hears a beat, yet you perceive a slow 10 hz wobble. it is made in the brainstem, in the superior olive, where signals from the two ears first meet. it only works with headphones, and with tones below about 1,000 hz.

    the effect was described by heinrich wilhelm dove in 1839. since the 1970s it has been sold as a way to steer brainwaves: a 10 hz beat for alpha calm, a 4 hz beat for theta, and so on.

    the evidence is mixed. some studies report small effects on anxiety, mood or attention, but reviews find the eeg rarely follows the beat in a consistent way, and many studies are small. it's an interesting perceptual trick, and some people find it relaxing, but it isn't a reliable dial for brain states.

    keep the volume low. the beat changes nothing about how loud the sound is.

    ( 05 · sleep-like states, awake )

    05 / 05

    some practices reach the edge of sleep without going over it.

    meditation

    more alpha, then theta

    settled, eyes-closed meditation usually raises alpha, and focused practice often adds theta over the front midline of the head. a 2004 study of long-term buddhist practitioners found unusually strong, widespread gamma during compassion meditation. none of this is sleep: awareness stays on.

    yoga nidra

    "yogic sleep"

    a guided relaxation done lying down, moving attention around the body. the eeg drifts from alpha toward theta, close to the border of n1, while the person stays aware of the voice. a small 2002 brain-scan study found more dopamine released during it.

    hypnagogia

    the n1 doorway

    the drifting images at sleep onset belong to n1. salvador dalí and others napped holding an object that would drop and wake them there. a 2021 study found people who slipped briefly into n1 were much more likely to spot a hidden shortcut in a maths task afterwards.

    lucid dreams

    awake inside rem

    in a lucid dream you know you're dreaming, sometimes well enough to signal to researchers with agreed eye movements. lucid rem shows more fast activity, around 40 hz, over the front of the brain than ordinary rem.
